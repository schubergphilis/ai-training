This page describes the AI Training project as a whole. It explains why the
project exists and what the site contains. It then shows how a lesson works
and which ideas about learning are behind that design. After that it tells how the project started,
how it grew, and how work on it happens now. The last sections describe the
agent harness that does most of the writing and the software that runs the
site.

## In short

AI Training is an open training suite for people who start to use
generative AI in their work. It has <AboutFigure of="areas" /> areas in
<AboutFigure of="groups" /> groups. The Foundations group (Concepts, Safety
and Using agents) is for anyone who does knowledge work. The Engineering
group (Coding with agents, Customizing agents and Building agents) is for
software engineers.

{/* source: site/src/data/groups.yaml; lesson counts are build-time figures */}

Each area has one course, and each course is a sequence of interactive
lessons with graded checkpoints. Each lesson ends in
one exercise that the learner does outside the page.

The site keeps the
learner's progress in their own browser and brings finished checkpoints
back for review on a fixed schedule. A learner can also study a lesson with
an AI tutor that runs in their own coding agent.

The site is a static website on GitHub Pages. It runs entirely in the
browser, without accounts or tracking. The content is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0 and the code
Apache-2.0, so others may copy and adapt both.

The project started on September 19, 2026. One maintainer runs it. AI coding

decides what gets built. Agents review every change, and the maintainer
reviews the changes that fall outside a standing approval. The site states this in its
footer: the content is co-authored by AI.

## Why the project exists

### People need a common starting point

The project starts from a view of how people use these tools. Generative
AI tools reached office work in a short time. Many people use a chat
assistant or a coding agent without any training in how these tools work
or fail. The project expects the result to be uneven: some people get
useful work out of the tools, and others share data they shouldn't, trust
answers they should check, or give up after one bad result.

A training suite gives a group of people the same words and the same basic
model of the tools. People can then discuss a result, a risk or a working
method with each other. The project defines its own words in a project
dictionary and uses them the same way on every page.

{/* source: docs/spec/S01-dictionary.md "Introduction" */}

### Safe use and real use go together

Research on automation has found the same thing for decades. When a machine
does most of a task, people stop checking its work, and they lose the skill
to take over when it fails (@Bainbridge 1983). Studies of automation bias
show that people accept a wrong answer from a system they trust, even when
the evidence in front of them says otherwise (@Parasuraman 2010). The
project assumes that AI assistants make both effects more likely, because
they answer fluently and with confidence.

So the suite teaches safety in the Foundations group, before any
engineering material, and it returns to verification in every area. The
Safety course covers which data a person may share with a tool and how far
to trust an answer. Later parts cover the risks of an agent that acts on
its own, and governance such as the EU AI Act.

{/* source: site/src/content/docs/safety/index.mdx; site/src/data/groups.yaml (Engineering assumes Foundations) */}

### Open content

The project is public, and its license lets anyone reuse it. An
organization can run the courses for its own staff or change a lesson to
fit its own tools. A person can study alone, with only a
browser. The license is CC BY-SA 4.0 for the content and Apache-2.0 for the
code.

The first license, on the first day, was CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. It was changed
the same evening. The "non-commercial" term made training inside a company
an unclear use, and it blocked the project from adapting material under
BY-SA, which Diátaxis uses (@Diátaxis).

{/* source: a2915086 "docs: relicense content to CC BY-SA 4.0, code to Apache-2.0; adopt Diátaxis" (2026-09-19), its commit message */}

The license also limits what the project may take from others. A lesson may
adapt text only from a source whose license allows it, such as Apache-2.0,
CC BY or CC BY-SA material. Other sources, such as Claude Academy courses
(@Academy ai-fluency-framework-foundations), DeepLearning.AI courses and
CS50 (@CS50x), are cited and linked. Their text is never copied.

{/* source: AGENTS.md "Content and licensing"; docs/agents/writing-a-lesson.md "Source licenses"; docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md "Source material" */}

### Topics, concepts and competencies

The site describes what it teaches with a small knowledge model. The model
is data, in YAML files next to the lessons, and the build reads it.

- A **topic** is one subject in one area, such as "Prompting" in Concepts.
  Topics link to the topics they build on. Each topic has a list of
  **concepts**, each with a one-paragraph definition. All of the concepts
  together make the glossary.
- A **competency** says what a learner can do after a course, as one
  sentence. It has **learning objectives**, each at a `base` or an `expert`
  level. Each objective has **behaviors**: a claim about what a competent
  person does, the reason for it, and an example.
- A lesson covers one topic and serves one or more objectives. A graded
  checkpoint tests one objective.

{/* source: docs/spec/S01-dictionary.md "Knowledge model"; docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md; docs/spec/S10-competency-authoring.md */}

## Inside a lesson

A lesson is one page. It is meant to take 10 to 25 minutes of reading and
checkpoints, plus the exercise. The data check warns when a lesson's
reading and checkpoints alone take more than 25 minutes. Each lesson has the same parts in the same
order, so that a learner knows where they are on any page.

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Lesson anatomy", "Lesson time"; site/src/lib/lesson-time.ts warnMinutes */}

### Two kinds of lesson

The project uses the Diátaxis model of documentation (@Diátaxis). That
model sorts a page by two questions. Does the reader act or think? Is the
reader studying or working? The answers give four kinds of page: tutorial,
explanation, how-to guide and reference.

Lessons are for study, so a lesson is a **tutorial** or an
**explanation**. A tutorial has the learner do something and see the
result early and often. It follows one path, in a setting the learner can
reset. Its rhythm is fixed: one or two paragraphs, then one example to run
or predict. An explanation builds understanding. It may compare options
and hold an opinion. The planned lessons, the ones with a page and the
ones still to write, are of these kinds:

The site builds reference pages from data: each topic page and the
glossary. The guides, such as the guide to running a workshop, are
hand-written how-to pages.

{/* source: docs/spec/S01-dictionary.md "Page kinds"; docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Choosing the page kind" */}

### The parts of a lesson

A lesson opens with one or two sentences in the present tense that say what
the lesson does. Then come the teaching sections. Some of these are
**pitfalls**, short boxes that show a common mistake and its cost. Graded
**checkpoints** sit between the teaching sections, close to the idea they
test. After the teaching comes one **exercise**, and then a **recap**. The
recap lists the takeaways and the objectives the learner can now meet, and
it holds the button that finishes the lesson. Some lessons end with one or
two **habits**.

{/* source: docs/spec/S01-dictionary.md "Section kinds"; docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Lesson anatomy" */}

<LessonAnatomy />

A lesson that teaches prompting shows prompts and responses in their own
styled blocks. Each block says which model produced the response and on
which date, or marks the exchange as illustrative when it was written by
hand. Model output changes over time, so the reader can judge how current
an example is.

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Prompts" (recorded and illustrative prompt blocks) */}

The rules for checkpoints are the same everywhere:

- Each checkpoint tests exactly one learning objective, and it names the
  concepts it uses.
- A wrong answer to a choice question shows feedback for that answer. A hint is a question that
  points the learner at their own mistake, and it never gives the answer.
- The lesson never reveals the answer. The learner can try again as often
  as they want, or skip the checkpoint. The site records a skip, and it
  doesn't count a skip as a pass.
- A checkpoint is graded all or nothing.
- Every checkpoint works with the keyboard alone.

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Checkpoints" */}

A multiple-choice question has a known weakness: a reader can often guess
the answer from the options alone. The longest option, the option with
careful hedges, or the option that repeats words from the question is often
the right one. A check looks at every choice, scenario and multiple-choice
checkpoint for such cues. It also fails a lesson where one position holds
the right answer in more than three quarters of its items. An author can
exempt an item only with a written reason.

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Checkpoints" (guessability); site/scripts/lib/guessability.mjs; mise run checkpoints */}

To see a real checkpoint, open the first checkpoint of the lesson
[How a language model works](/concepts/how-models-work/#what-the-model-does).
A checkpoint only works inside its lesson, because a pass becomes part of
your progress record.

### Examples that run

Many lessons in the Engineering group show code and the output it prints.
That output is never typed by hand. Each example is a small program in the
repository, and a build check runs it and compares what it prints with
what the page shows. The check runs every example on two versions of
Python, the current release and an older version that some learners still
have. A change in Python, or a mistake in a lesson, fails the check before
the page is published.

The site has <AboutFigure of="examples-run" /> such examples. Some are
graded **predict** checkpoints, where the learner first guesses the output
and then runs the code to check the guess. The others are worked examples
that the learner reads or runs.

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Examples"; site/scripts/check-examples.mjs; .mise.toml (Python 3.14.7 and 3.9.25, as of 2026-10-09) */}

### Exercises

Each lesson ends with one exercise. The learner does it outside the page,
with their own tools, in a setting they can reset, such as a practice
repository. The exercise brief says what to do and what a finished result
looks like. Then the learner compares their work with a model answer. No
one grades the exercise. It ends with one reflection question.

There are two kinds of exercise. In a **do** exercise the learner produces
something, such as an instruction file or a test. In a **judge** exercise
the learner gets a result, such as an agent's output or a pull request, and
assesses it. Some exercises add a one-line stretch goal for
learners who want more.

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Exercises"; docs/spec/S01-dictionary.md "Exercise" */}

### Routing between learners

People start a course with different knowledge. The site writes each lesson
once, and it changes the route through the material for each learner.

Each lesson declares the objectives it serves and the objectives it
assumes. If a learner fails or skips a checkpoint that depends on an
assumed objective, the lesson shows a card that points to the section where
that objective is taught. If a learner passes every checkpoint at the first
try, the lesson shows a card that suggests where to go next. The site never
locks a lesson.

{/* source: docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md "Differentiation by routing"; site/src/components/lesson/RouteAhead.astro */}

In the Engineering group the learner also picks a comfort level, an idea
the project took from CS50 (@CS50x). At the "less comfortable" level the
lesson shows the card with the sections that teach what it assumes from
the start, and new review
items come back sooner. At the "more comfortable" level the lesson starts
with a short skills check. It skips what the learner already knows and
offers extensions. The level changes the route and never the content.

{/* source: docs/spec/S01-dictionary.md "Comfort level"; docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md "Differentiation by routing"; docs/spec/S05-spaced-review.md (entry stage by comfort level) */}

### After the lesson

When a learner finishes a lesson, the site schedules its review items and
its habits, and adds the lesson to the learner's reference.

**Spaced review.** Most graded checkpoints of a finished lesson become
review items. Repair checkpoints, and ones the author marks as one-off,
don't. Each course has a review page. An item comes back 1, 3, 7, 21
and 60 days after the learner last passed it. With a pass it moves to the
next step, and with a fail it goes back to the first step. After the fifth pass
the item retires. A session asks at most 12 items. Many checkpoints have
alternate versions that only the review page asks, so that a review tests
the idea rather than the memory of the wording. The sidebar shows how many
items are due per course.

{/* source: docs/spec/S05-spaced-review.md "Schedule", "Where reviews surface", "Alternates" */}

**Habits.** A habit is a small task to do in your own work, in the form
"The next time you do X, do Y". It comes back 1, 3 and 7 days after the
lesson. After that the site stops showing it.

{/* source: docs/spec/S07-habits.md */}

the day it is due.

**The learner's reference.** A topic page has a short summary of the

shows what is due when the learner comes back to it.

### Your progress stays with you

The site stores all progress in one record in the learner's browser, in
local storage. The site sends nothing to a server. The progress page can export
the record to a file and import it in another browser. The record has a
version number, and the site converts an older record when it loads it.

A static site can't hide its answers, because the answers are in the page.
The site trusts the learner not to look up the answer. The record is for the
learner, and it grades no one.

{/* source: docs/spec/S04-progress-record.md "Principles", "Storage", "Export and import"; SECURITY.md "Scope" */}

### The tutor

A learner can study a lesson with an AI tutor in their own coding agent.
The tutor is a skill that the learner installs once. When it starts, it
fetches its instructions and the lesson from the site. It then gives hints
and asks questions. It doesn't give the answers to checkpoints or
exercises, and it stays on the lesson the learner names. When the learner
pastes their progress export, the tutor first asks one recall question for
a review that is due.

{/* source: docs/spec/S08-tutor-skill.md; .claude/skills/ai-tutor/SKILL.md; site/src/tutor/instructions.md */}

The design follows the AI tutor that CS50 built for its students, which
gives hints and guides the student to the answer (@Liu 2024b). The project
tests the tutor with a fixed set of learner questions, each with what the
tutor must and must not do. A change to the tutor that makes it give an
answer away doesn't ship.

{/* source: docs/agents/tutor-eval.md (hand-graded queries, must-not failure counts double, "a change that adds one doesn't ship") */}

## The ideas about learning behind the design

The project draws on a small number of ideas from learning research and
from other learning platforms. The specs state each idea in their own words,
with the reason for it, and most of the ideas came to the project through
other platforms. This section describes each idea as the project uses it,
and names research that supports it. The numbers in the design, such as
the review intervals, are first guesses. The project has no data about
real learners yet.

### Retrieval practice and spaced review

The spaced-review spec starts from one claim: interactive lessons alone
produce recognition, and recall needs practice. A learner who reads a page
can recognize its ideas later, which is less than being able to recall
them. So the site asks a learner to retrieve each idea again, after
growing intervals. It also uses alternate questions. With those, the
learner recalls the idea and not the wording of one question.

Memory research supports this design. In one study, students who
took a test on a text remembered more of it a week later than students who
spent the same time reading it again (@Roediger 2006). A review of 317
experiments found that practice spread out over time gives better
long-term recall than the same practice in one session (@Cepeda 2006). A
later review of ten study techniques rated these two as the most useful,
and rereading as one of the least (@Dunlosky 2013).

{/* source: docs/spec/S05-spaced-review.md "Introduction"; Roediger 2006 (restudy won at five minutes, testing at two days and one week); Cepeda 2006 (review of 317 experiments in 184 articles); Dunlosky 2013 (practice testing and distributed practice rated high utility, rereading low) */}

### Prediction before the result

The project assumes that a learner who predicts a result before seeing it
pays more attention to the result. When the prediction is wrong, the
difference shows where their model of the system is wrong. The predict checkpoint uses this. Code
examples follow a fixed rhythm: one idea, then one example. The project
took both from Execute Program, an online platform that teaches
programming with short, executable examples. Research supports the first
part: an attempt to answer before seeing the answer helps later recall,
even when the attempt fails (@Kornell 2009).

{/* source: exploration note 10 (Execute Program), d7ca2629; docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Examples"; Kornell 2009 (unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance later learning) */}

### Design from the outcomes

The design starts from what the learner can do afterwards, and works back
to the lessons. Course designers call this backward design (@Wiggins 2005).
It starts from the results a course wants and the evidence that would
show them, and plans the teaching last. The competencies, objectives and
behaviors say what a learner can do. Each checkpoint tests one objective. So the site can show
which objectives a learner has evidence for, and a reviewer can check
whether a lesson tests what it claims to teach.

{/* source: docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md; docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Checkpoints" (one objective per checkpoint, at least one per served objective) */}

### One text with several routes

Writing a separate course for each level of learner costs too much, and the
versions drift apart. The project writes each lesson once. Routing cards,
comfort levels and the skills check adapt the route through it. The model
for this was a classroom method with three paths: review a prerequisite,
work on the current objective, or take an extension.

{/* source: docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md "Differentiation by routing" (Brilliant's classroom model) */}

### Hints that ask questions

The project assumes that a hint that gives the answer ends the learner's own thinking. In
this project a hint asks the learner a question about their own mistake. The
tutor follows the same rule. The idea comes from CS50's AI tutor
(@Liu 2024b). Research on tutoring calls this kind of help scaffolding: the
tutor takes over the parts of a task that the learner can't do yet, and
leaves the rest to the learner (@Wood 1976).

{/* source: docs/spec/S03-lesson-authoring.md "Checkpoints"; docs/spec/S08-tutor-skill.md; docs/spec/S02-topic-map.md "Source material" (CS50) */}

### From the page to the job

People often don't use at work what they read on a page. Exercises use the
learner's own tools in a practice setting. Habits are closer to the job:
they ask the learner to change one thing in their real work, and come back
to ask whether they did. The aim is that a lesson changes what a learner
does, as well as what they know. The habit form, "The next time you do X,
do Y", is a plan of the kind that psychologists call an implementation
intention. Such plans make it more likely that people act on a goal
(@Gollwitzer 1999).

{/* source: docs/spec/S07-habits.md "Introduction" */}

### Plain language

Many readers have English as a second language. The project keeps the
concepts at a professional level and the language simple. It prefers common
words and plain sentences, and a lesson expands each abbreviation on
first use. A prose linter checks every page for wordy, vague or
hard-to-read text, and the reading-time estimate assumes a slower reading
speed than a native speaker has.

{/* source: AGENTS.md "Voice"; .vale.ini (Readability, write-good, proselint, Google, House); site/src/lib/lesson-time.ts proseWpm (180, below the measured 238) */}

## How work happens now

The project has one maintainer and no other human contributors so far.
The maintainer decides what to build and what to accept. Agents write the
lessons, the code, the tests and most issue text, and other agents review
that work. This section describes the process as the repository documents
it, and as the issue history shows it.

{/* source: git shortlog -sne HEAD (one human author, as of 2026-10-09); docs/agents/orchestration.md; docs/agents/meta-orchestration.md */}

### Everything starts as an issue

Every piece of work is a GitHub issue. An issue has one label for the kind
of change: `content`, `code` or `harness`. The last one covers the agent
setup itself. A triage pass with the maintainer leaves every issue in one
of three states. It is ready for an agent, with its decisions written down,
or ready for a human, with one named action, or it is closed with a reason.

A follow-up that a review finds, and an improvement the maintainer defers,
become new issues before a session ends. Agents keep open work in issues,
and never in a chat log or a session record.

{/* source: docs/agents/issue-tracker.md "Labels"; docs/agents/triage.md; AGENTS.md "Process" */}

### Waves of agents

Most work happens in **runs**. A run is one session in which the maintainer
starts a dispatcher with the `/wave` command. The dispatcher picks a few
ready issues and starts a **wave lead** for them. The wave lead then starts
one **builder** agent per issue. Each builder works in its own copy of the
repository, on its own branch. When a builder pushes its branch, the lead
starts a **reviewer** agent for it. For a lesson, the reviewer compares the
prose with the authoring guide and checks the license of each source and
each factual claim. For code, the reviewer reads the change and runs the
checks. Builders and reviewers never talk to each
other directly. The lead passes the review to the builder, and the builder
fixes what the review found.

{/* source: docs/agents/meta-orchestration.md "Layers"; .claude/agents/builder.md, lesson-reviewer.md, code-reviewer.md, wave-lead.md */}

<WaveDiagram />

When every branch in the wave is approved, the lead combines them on one
wave branch. It runs the full check suite there and opens one pull
request. The maintainer has given standing approval for a wave that passes
every check, so the lead may merge such a wave without asking. That
approval never covers a wave that changes the agent setup itself, as the
harness section explains.

{/* source: docs/agents/meta-orchestration.md "Standing approval", "Harness runs" */}

Each run gets an animal name in alphabetical order, from Axolotl to
Zebra and then on from Alpaca. The run has its own GitHub issue, which
records its waves and its state, so that a run can resume after an
interruption. As of October 9, 2026, there have been 30 run issues under
28 names. One name has two runs, and one run has a follow-up.

{/* source: .claude/skills/wave/run-names.txt; gh issue list --label dispatcher-run --state all (30, Axolotl #358 to Bison #746, as of 2026-10-09) */}

### Why there are two layers

At first one coordinating session managed all builders and reviewers
itself. Each issue cost that session about twenty thousand tokens of its
context, and with more than a hundred ready issues one context could not
hold the work. The wave lead takes that load, and the dispatcher keeps
only a short report per wave.

{/* source: docs/agents/meta-orchestration.md "Why a second layer" (about 20k tokens per issue; 113 ready issues on 2026-09-23) */}

### What the maintainer does

Agents are set up to ask the maintainer as little as possible. They
decide routine matters themselves and report what they decided. They ask
the maintainer only when a decision touches a spec, a project rule, the
scope of the work or its cost. A question to the maintainer holds one
decision in plain words, with one recommendation. Merging a pull request
outside a green wave needs the maintainer's explicit word.

{/* source: AGENTS.md "Asking the maintainer", "Interactive sessions" */}

### Reviewing the process itself

The process asks for a harness review once a week and after each change
of model or orchestration. Two have run so far. A harness review looks at where time, tokens and the maintainer's attention
went. It reads the git history, the issues and the agents' session logs,
and it compares the project's own way of working with what its lessons
teach. The findings the maintainer picks become issues. The reviews are
public as issues. The full reports stay outside the repository, because
session logs can hold private material.

{/* source: docs/agents/harness-review.md; .claude/skills/harness-review/SKILL.md; issues #385 and #726 */}

## Harness engineering

An agent harness is everything around a model that decides what it does:
its instructions, its tools, its permissions, the checks its work must
pass, and the way its work is reviewed and merged. The lessons in the
Customizing agents course teach the same kind of tools.

{/* source: docs/agents/*.md; .claude/; site/src/content/docs/customizing-agents/ */}

### The parts of the harness

- **Instructions.** One instruction file at the root of the repository
  holds the rules every agent reads first. Process documents for agents
  describe lesson writing, testing, the issue tracker, triage,
  orchestration, completion and supply chain, one subject each.
- **Agent definitions.** The repository defines agents for each role:
  builder, code reviewer, lesson reviewer, security reviewer and wave
  lead. Each file sets
  the model, the effort level, the maximum number of turns and the tools
  the agent may use. A reviewer can't edit files, and the lesson reviewer
  has no shell at all.
- **Skills.** The repository has skills for the wave dispatcher, triage,
  the harness review and the learner's tutor. Only a person can start the
  first three.
- **Hooks.** A hook is a script that runs before or after an agent's tool
  call (@Claude Code hooks). A guard hook checks every shell command an agent wants to run.
- **Checks.** One command runs every check, and every push and merge must
  pass it.

{/* source: AGENTS.md; ls docs/agents (11 docs); ls .claude/agents (5); ls .claude/skills (ai-tutor, harness-review, triage, wave); .claude/settings.json; .claude/hooks/README.md; scripts/agent_hooks.py */}

### Checking the prose

Agents write prose with patterns that readers learn to recognize: stacked
lists of three, slogans, sentences built on "not X, but Y", and favorite
words. The project runs the prose linter Vale on every page. It uses
several public rule packages, including one made for typical AI writing,
plus rules of its own. Each rule was evaluated one by one before it
was turned on. Pages of the site get a stricter level, where most of
those rules are errors that block a change. A spelling check in American English
runs next to it.

{/* source: .vale.ini (write-good, proselint, Readability, Google, ai-tells, House); docs/prose/README.md; 42bff6b3, 2eadb46f (rule-by-rule evaluation); cspell.json */}

### Checking the work

The full check suite runs on every pull request and before every merge.

- Formatting and linting of the site code and the Python scripts
- Type checks of the site and the Python code
- Unit tests of the site code and the Python scripts, each with a floor of
  80% test coverage
- The prose and spelling checks
- The runnable code examples, on two Python versions
- A check of the data files against each other and against the pages
- A build of the whole site, which fails on any broken internal link,
  unknown citation or broken checkpoint
- Checks of the published data files, the tutor bundles and the security
  policy of every page
- Browser tests of real lesson pages

{/* source: .mise.toml [tasks.fast] and [tasks.ci]; docs/agents/testing.md */}

### Supply chain

Every dependency of the site and of the Python tools is pinned to an exact
version, and the lock files are in the repository. The GitHub Actions that
the workflows use are pinned to full commit hashes. New releases of a tool wait seven days before
the project uses them. A scanner checks the lock files for known
vulnerabilities on every push and once a week. An exception for a known
vulnerability expires after at most 30 days, and an agent must ask the
maintainer before it adds one.

{/* source: docs/agents/supply-chain.md; .mise.toml (minimum_release_age 7d); pyproject.toml (exclude-newer); .github/dependabot.yml (cooldown 7); osv-scanner.toml; tests/test_osv_ignores.py */}

## Software architecture

### A static site

The site is built with Astro and its documentation theme Starlight, and
published on GitHub Pages. It has no server code. A push to the main branch
builds the site and publishes it.

{/* source: site/package.json; .github/workflows/deploy.yml */}

### Content as data and pages

The site's sources are data files and pages.

- **Data files** in YAML describe the groups, areas, topics, competencies,
  courses, lesson plans, framework alignments and the bibliography. The
  build loads them as typed collections with a strict schema, so a
  misspelled field fails the build. A separate check compares the data
  with the pages. For example, it checks that each lesson plan has a page
  and that each assumed objective exists.
- **Pages** in MDX, which is Markdown with components. A lesson page has no
  metadata of its own. Its title, kind and objectives come from its lesson
  plan file, so the data has one source.

{/* source: site/src/content.config.ts; docs/spec/S09-area-authoring.md, S10, S11; site/scripts/check-data.mjs */}

### The build

The build turns the sources into HTML in a few steps.

- A checkpoint reader parses every checkpoint and fails on a malformed one.
- A terms plugin links the first mention of a concept to its glossary
  entry.
- A citations plugin turns each citation into a numbered reference and adds
  the References section.
- A link plugin adds the base path of the site to internal links.
- A link validator fails the build on a broken internal link.

{/* source: site/astro.config.mjs markdown.remarkPlugins and rehypePlugins; site/plugins/ */}

The build writes three outputs. The HTML site is for people. Markdown
copies of the pages, an index for AI agents and the data tree as JSON are
for agents that read the site for a person. Per-lesson bundles are for the
tutor.

<ArchitectureDiagram />

{/* source: site/astro.config.mjs; site/src/pages/ (llms.txt.ts, data/, [...page]/index.md.ts); docs/spec/S12-agent-readable-site.md; docs/spec/S08-tutor-skill.md */}

### In the browser

Small scripts make the pages interactive. They grade checkpoints, show
routing cards, schedule reviews and habits, and color the maps. They read
and write one progress record in local storage. No script sends data
anywhere.

A Content Security Policy on every page allows scripts only from the site
itself. A build check rejects a page without the policy, an inline script
without a known hash, and any script or stylesheet from another site. The
site serves its own fonts. A page load makes no request to a font
service.

{/* source: site/src/scripts/; site/scripts/lib/csp.mjs; site/scripts/check-bundles.mjs (checkCsp); docs/spec/S14-visual-identity.md (self-hosted font) */}

## Limits and open questions

The project is young, and some of its design rests on guesses.

- No group of learners has finished a course yet. The time estimates and
  the review intervals are first guesses, to tune once there is data.
- Some parts of the design are in the specs but not on the site yet,
  among them learning paths per role and quizzes per course.
- The content is written by AI agents. Each change is reviewed by an agent
  that is asked to check every factual claim against a public source.
  Mistakes are still possible. A learner who finds one can open an issue
  on GitHub.
- Many lessons teach specific tools, and those tools change often. Lessons
  whose facts change, such as those about a product or a law, record the
  date their sources were last checked and a date by which to check them
  again.
